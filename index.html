From Gridlock to Insight: A Visual Approach to the Urban Traffic Crisis

Carolyn Lee, Lasya Muthyam, Daniel Wang, Gavin Crisologo, Nathan James

Introduction

Problem: Traffic congestion is one of the most persistent problems facing modern cities. As urban populations grow and transportation demand increases, cities experience longer travel times, higher fuel consumption, increased air pollution, and reduced transportation reliability. However, congestion is not caused solely by the number of vehicles on the road — the structure of a city’s street network also strongly influences how traffic moves and where bottlenecks emerge.

Research Question: How do different urban street-network designs influence traffic flow, travel efficiency, and congestion patterns?

Approach: This project compares four major street-network patterns — grid, radial, linear, and curvilinear/suburban systems — using interactive traffic visualizations built from real-world roadway data. Each visualization encodes vehicle speed, travel time, and traffic intensity to show how congestion distributes differently across urban layouts.

Dataset: Our visualizations use data from the TomTom Traffic Stats API, which provides street-level traffic information including average vehicle speeds, travel times, and roadway congestion statistics collected from anonymized navigation data.

Visualization Overview

In each of the visualizations below, we show how a specific street pattern influences traffic density by visualizing the street layout, number of cars, vehicle speeds, and travel times.

  • Street Layout 🗺️: The underlying street layout is visualized to provide context for the traffic patterns on the map.
  • Number of Cars 🚗: Each of the circles on the map represents one car driving on that particular road segment during the analyzed time period.
  • Vehicle Speed 🏃: The colors of the circles represent the speed of each vehicle, with red indicating slow speeds and pure green representing the speed limit.
  • Travel Time ⏱️: The size of the circles on the map represents travel time, with larger circles showing longer travel times.

Conclusion & Next Steps

Conclusion

This project shows that traffic congestion is not only a product of how many cars are on the road, but also how those cars are distributed by the structure of the street network. Grid systems provide many alternate paths, helping traffic spread across the network, but frequent intersections can reduce speeds. Radial systems create direct routes to central areas, but they are vulnerable to bottlenecks where major roads converge. Linear systems support efficient movement along a main corridor, but disruptions can affect the entire route. Curvilinear suburban systems reduce local neighborhood traffic, but often push congestion onto collector and arterial roads.

Overall, the visualizations suggest that no single road pattern is universally “best.” Each design reflects a tradeoff between speed, accessibility, redundancy, safety, and neighborhood livability. By comparing these layouts visually, users can see how urban design decisions shape congestion patterns in ways that raw traffic numbers alone may not reveal.

Next Steps

  • Add more cities: Compare multiple examples of each road type to avoid drawing conclusions from only one location per pattern.
  • Analyze more time periods: Include morning rush hour, evening rush hour, weekends, and late-night traffic to show how congestion changes throughout the day.
  • Improve traffic intensity metrics: Normalize sampled vehicles by total road-network length instead of geographic bounding-box area to reduce shape-based bias.
  • Add interaction filters: Let users toggle between speed, travel time, and traffic intensity layers directly on each map.
  • Connect findings to planning decisions: Use the results to discuss where cities might benefit from alternate routes, transit investment, signal optimization, or traffic calming.

Grid Pattern

Grid street networks use perpendicular streets and frequent intersections, creating many route choices between origins and destinations. They are common in dense downtown areas because they support navigation, walkability, and multimodal travel.

Pros Cons
Many alternate routes help distribute traffic. Frequent intersections can create stop-and-go delay.
Easy navigation with predictable blocks and directions. Signal density can lower average corridor speeds.
Supports walking, cycling, and transit access well. Less adaptable to irregular terrain like hills or rivers.

Radial Pattern

Radial networks organize major roads like spokes leading to central hubs, often linked by circles or ring roads. This design gives fast direct access to downtown cores and major institutions, but it can also create recurring bottlenecks at the hub.

Pros Cons
Direct routes to central districts reduce travel distance. Convergence at hubs creates frequent peak-hour bottlenecks.
Clear hierarchy of major corridors and connectors. Limited alternate routes for cross-town movement.
Ring roads can help redistribute some inbound traffic. Complex merging and circulation at central circles.

Linear Pattern

Linear street systems follow a dominant corridor, often shaped by geography such as valleys, coastlines, or rail alignments. They can move traffic efficiently along one axis, but reduced redundancy makes congestion harder to avoid.

Pros Cons
Efficient long-distance movement along a primary axis. Fewer reroute options when primary links are congested.
Aligns well with corridor transit infrastructure. Localized disruptions can affect a large share of trips.
Simple structure for corridor-focused urban growth. Can create bottlenecks at feeder-road junctions.
Transition to Suburban Street Networks

Unlike dense urban systems that prioritize connectivity and centralized movement, suburban layouts prioritize residential separation, reduced through-traffic, and lower-density development.

Curvilinear/Suburban Pattern

Curvilinear suburban layouts use winding local roads and branching neighborhood structures to reduce through-traffic in residential areas. These networks improve neighborhood calm but often increase car dependency.

Pros Cons
Low through-traffic improves neighborhood quiet and safety. Limited connectivity can increase trip distance and delay.
Residential streets feel private and less congested. Higher reliance on collector and arterial roads.
Well suited for low-density suburban living goals. Transit and non-car access are often less efficient.
