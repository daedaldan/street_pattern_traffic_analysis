From Gridlock to Insight: A Visual Approach to the Urban Traffic Crisis

Carolyn Lee, Lasya Muthyam, Daniel Wang, Gavin Crisologo, Nathan James

Introduction

Traffic congestion is a significant problem that is associated with longer travel times, more traffic accidents, and increased air pollution. These impacts can cause further issues, such as higher costs of goods from increased shipping times, people missing important travel connections or medical appointments, and higher rates of asthma and respiratory disease.

However, designing better city network hierarchies and street layouts can alleviate urban congestion by improving traffic flow. We want to visualize how different street patterns influence traffic density by creating interactive visualizations that allow the viewer to better understand how urban planning can improve traffic congestion and density.

All of our visualizations use data from the TomTom Traffic Stats API, which provides detailed statistics on travel times, travel speeds, route accessibility, and congestion bottlenecks using anonymized data gathered from millions of navigation devices. Because the dataset provides hourly records of street-level traffic data from cities across the world, it is a significant source of data that enables us to provide a thorough and detailed analysis on how street patterns influence traffic density in different geographical regions and times of day.

Visualization Overview

In each of the visualizations below, we show how a specific street pattern influences traffic density by visualizing the street layout, number of cars, vehicle speeds, and travel times.

  • Street Layout 🗺️: The underlying street layout is visualized to provide context for the traffic patterns on the map.
  • Number of Cars 🚗: Each of the circles on the map represents one car driving on that particular road segment during the analyzed time period.
  • Vehicle Speed 🏃: The colors of the circles represent the speed of each vehicle, with red indicating slow speeds and pure green representing the speed limit.
  • Travel Time ⏱️: The size of the circles on the map represents travel time, with larger circles showing longer travel times.

Traffic Pattern Rankings

These summary cards compare each street pattern using weighted traffic statistics from the TomTom data. Average speed is normalized by each road segment’s speed limit, travel time is weighted by the number of sampled cars, and car density estimates sampled vehicles per square mile.

1. Grid Pattern

The grid layout, where streets intersect at right angles, is the most common street pattern and is often found in cities with dense downtowns, such as Manhattan, Chicago, and San Francisco. Grid layouts provide a variety of features that help reduce traffic congestion. For example, they help disperse traffic by providing a variety of routes from one place to another. If traffic is congested in one area, it is easy for drivers to find another route to the same destination that avoids the congested area.

Grid layouts also have high walkability and make it easier to implement cycling paths, so people are more likely to walk and ride bikes, which also helps decrease traffic congestion. Traffic flow is predictable as well, and it is easy to provide directions in terms of cardinal directions.

Nevertheless, there are distinct downsides of grid layouts compared to other street patterns. It can be easy for traffic congestion to occur at intersections, since frequent intersections at every block increase the number of stop signs or traffic signals, which decreases overall travel speeds. Furthermore, grid layouts do not adapt well to natural terrain, such as rivers and hills.

An archetypical example of the grid layout is Midtown Manhattan. We have visualized the traffic patterns in Midtown Manhattan from W 40th St to W 59th St and 5th Ave to 8th Ave below. The abundance of traffic signals at every block significantly reduces overall vehicle speeds by forcing cars to frequently slow down and stop, as represented by the plethora of red dots across the map. However, the presence of streets where vehicles have faster speeds and shorter travel times, such as 8th Ave, 6th Ave, and Central Park South are also represented by a larger number of smaller green dots at these locations. Through the grid layout, these streets provide travelers with alternate routes to their destinations that avoid the more congested areas. The use of numbered streets, which is commonly found in grid layouts, also makes navigation easier for commuters.



2. Radial Pattern

Radial street patterns are characterized by roads that extend outward from a central point, often forming a spoke-like structure. These roads are typically connected by circular intersections or ring roads, creating a system where traffic is directed toward key hubs. This type of layout is commonly found in cities that emphasize centralized planning, such as Washington, D.C.

One of the defining features of radial street patterns is their ability to provide efficient access to central locations. Major avenues often lead directly to important landmarks, business districts, or government centers, reducing travel distance for commuters heading into the city. However, this same structure can lead to significant congestion, as multiple high-capacity roads converge at a limited number of intersections.

Radial layouts also influence how traffic moves throughout the city. Traffic tends to flow inward toward the center during the morning and outward in the evening, creating predictable congestion patterns. While ring roads can help redistribute traffic, bottlenecks frequently occur at central hubs and major intersections where radial and connecting roads meet.

Dupont Circle is a good example of a radial street pattern within Washington, D.C as several major avenues, including Massachusetts Avenue, Connecticut Avenue, and New Hampshire Avenue, converge at a central circular intersection. This creates a highly connected but complex traffic environment. The radial design allows vehicles to reach the city center efficiently from multiple directions. However, because these roads intersect at a single point, Dupont Circle experiences significant congestion, particularly during peak commuting hours. The circular intersection further complicates traffic flow, as vehicles must navigate merging lanes and continuous movement patterns. Unlike grid layouts, which offer multiple alternate routes, the radial structure limits flexibility. Drivers traveling across the city often must pass through or around central hubs like Dupont Circle, increasing travel time and contributing to congestion buildup.



3. Linear Pattern

Linear street patterns are defined by roads that run primarily in parallel lines, often following a natural corridor such as a river valley, railway, or mountainous terrain. Unlike radial or grid systems, linear layouts tend to stretch in one dominant direction, creating a long, narrow urban form. This type of structure is commonly found in cities where geography strongly influences development, such as Wuppertal.

One of the defining features of a linear road network is its efficiency along a single axis. Major roads act as primary arteries, allowing traffic to flow smoothly from one end of the city to the other. In Wuppertal, this pattern follows the Wupper River valley, where roads, rail lines, and development are concentrated in a tight corridor. This creates a highly accessible system for movement along the length of the city, often supported by strong public transportation infrastructure.

However, this same structure can limit flexibility. Because most roads run in parallel, there are fewer alternative routes when congestion occurs. If a primary road becomes blocked or overloaded, traffic has limited options for rerouting. This can lead to bottlenecks, especially at key intersections or where smaller connecting roads feed into the main corridor.

Linear layouts also shape traffic patterns in a unique way. Instead of converging toward a central hub, traffic flows tend to move back and forth along the same corridor. Peak congestion often occurs along the main route rather than at a single central point. This creates a more distributed, but still concentrated, form of traffic buildup—less chaotic than radial systems, but potentially just as slow during high-demand periods.

A well-known example of this structure in Wuppertal is the corridor beneath the Wuppertal Suspension Railway (Schwebebahn), which runs directly above the Wupper River. The roads below largely mirror this path, forming a continuous linear network through the city. This alignment allows for efficient movement across long distances, as multiple transportation modes—cars, buses, and the suspended railway—operate along the same axis. However, because so much of the city’s infrastructure is concentrated in this narrow corridor, disruptions in one part of the system can ripple across the entire network, leading to delays and congestion along much of the route.



4. Curvilinear/Suburban Pattern

Curvilinear (or suburban) street patterns are an iconic component of American middle-class suburbia, with an emphasis on winding roads and cul-de-sacs, to improve privacy and offer a countryside-esque living aesthetic. They are easily recognizable by the use of roads that "branch out" to produce sprawling suburban areas that are somewhat isolated from the surrounding area. Curvilinear street designs are often found in post-war suburbs in the U.S. and Canada, such as Fair Lawn, New Jersey.

Neighborhoods that follow a curvilinear design are desirable for their reduced traffic (and corresponding traffic volume) thanks to the lack of thoroughfares, improved privacy, and organic aesthetic. The sprawling design also can make it easier for guests to find parking in these neighborhoods.

However, suburban layouts come with their own downsides. While they are walkable within the neighborhood, they often are designed in a way that makes it difficult to access the surrounding city without a personal car. Public transportation also tends to be less efficient in these areas, which can make them notably isolated from local shops and services. This also tends to create a dependency on driving over walking and biking.

Fair Lawn, New Jersey is a classic example of a curvilinear street design. It is notable for its adherence to a hierarchy of arterial roads (with high traffic density), collector roads (which connect residential streets to arterial roads) and residential streets (designed just for local neighborhood traffic). From the visualization below, we can clearly see that there are generally extremely low travel times within neighborhoods, but notable traffic hotspots on the collector and primary arterial roads. Therefore, we can see that curvilinear street designs can make for peaceful, low-traffic density living environments, but result in challenging traffic conditions the farther you get from strictly local suburban streets.

5. Cul-de-Sac Network Pattern


Cul‑de‑sacs are dead‑end streets branching off collector roads. Think of American suburbs and gated communities. They are designed to reduce through traffic and create quiet, low‑traffic residential areas. However, they can lead to increased traffic congestion on collector roads, as all vehicles must enter and exit through a limited number of access points. This can create bottlenecks during peak hours and limit alternative routes for drivers.
