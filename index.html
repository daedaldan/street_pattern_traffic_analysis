From Gridlock to Insight: A Visual Approach to the Urban Traffic Crisis

Carolyn Lee, Lasya Muthyam, Daniel Wang, Gavin Crisologo, Nathan James

Introduction

Traffic congestion is a significant problem that is associated with longer travel times, more traffic accidents, and increased air pollution. These impacts can cause further issues, such as higher costs of goods from increased shipping times, people missing important travel connections or medical appointments, and higher rates of asthma and respiratory disease.

However, designing better city network hierarchies and street layouts can alleviate urban congestion by improving traffic flow. We want to visualize how different street patterns influence traffic density by creating interactive visualizations that allow the viewer to better understand how urban planning can improve traffic congestion and density.

All of our visualizations use data from the TomTom Traffic Stats API, which provides detailed statistics on travel times, travel speeds, route accessibility, and congestion bottlenecks using anonymized data gathered from millions of navigation devices. Because the dataset provides hourly records of street-level traffic data from cities across the world, it is a significant source of data that enables us to provide a thorough and detailed analysis on how street patterns influence traffic density in different geographical regions and times of day.

1. Grid Pattern

Grid street networks use perpendicular streets and frequent intersections, creating many route choices between origins and destinations. They are common in dense downtown areas because they support navigation, walkability, and multimodal travel. In this map, Midtown Manhattan shows both the benefit of alternate paths and the cost of frequent signalized intersections. The pattern works best where connectivity and accessibility are priorities, but it can slow traffic at busy crossing points.


Grid Pattern: Quick Pros and Cons
Pros Cons
Many alternate routes help distribute traffic. Frequent intersections can create stop-and-go delay.
Easy navigation with predictable blocks and directions. Signal density can lower average corridor speeds.
Supports walking, cycling, and transit access well. Less adaptable to irregular terrain like hills or rivers.

2. Radial Pattern

Radial networks organize major roads like spokes leading to central hubs, often linked by circles or ring roads. This design gives fast direct access to downtown cores and major institutions, which is useful for centralized cities like Washington, D.C. In the Dupont Circle example, converging avenues create strong connectivity but also recurring bottlenecks at the hub. Radial layouts are effective for center-focused travel demand but can struggle when cross-city traffic needs flexibility.


Radial Pattern: Quick Pros and Cons
Pros Cons
Direct routes to central districts reduce travel distance. Convergence at hubs creates frequent peak-hour bottlenecks.
Clear hierarchy of major corridors and connectors. Limited alternate routes for cross-town movement.
Ring roads can help redistribute some inbound traffic. Complex merging and circulation at central circles.

3. Linear Pattern

Linear street systems follow a dominant corridor, often shaped by geography such as valleys, coastlines, or rail alignments. They can move traffic efficiently along one axis and pair well with transit lines that run in the same direction, as seen in Wuppertal. The tradeoff is reduced redundancy: if a key segment is congested or blocked, detour options are limited. This pattern is best for elongated cities with strong corridor-based travel demand.


Linear Pattern: Quick Pros and Cons
Pros Cons
Efficient long-distance movement along a primary axis. Fewer reroute options when primary links are congested.
Aligns well with corridor transit infrastructure. Localized disruptions can affect a large share of trips.
Simple structure for corridor-focused urban growth. Can create bottlenecks at feeder-road junctions.

4. Curvilinear/Suburban Pattern

Curvilinear suburban layouts use winding local roads and branching neighborhood structures to reduce through-traffic in residential areas. They often improve privacy, neighborhood calm, and local safety by limiting direct cut-through routes. In the Fair Lawn example, internal streets are generally low-stress, while collectors and arterials absorb heavier congestion. These networks are useful for residential comfort, but they typically increase car dependency and reduce regional connectivity.


Curvilinear/Suburban Pattern: Quick Pros and Cons
Pros Cons
Low through-traffic improves neighborhood quiet and safety. Limited connectivity can increase trip distance and delay.
Residential streets feel private and less congested. Higher reliance on collector and arterial roads.
Well suited for low-density suburban living goals. Transit and non-car access are often less efficient.

5. Cul-de-Sac Network Pattern

Cul-de-sac networks rely on dead-end residential streets that feed into a smaller set of collector roads. This design minimizes non-local traffic inside neighborhoods and creates quieter local environments. In places like Carderock Springs, most congestion shifts outward to collectors and arterial access points. Cul-de-sac systems are useful for residential privacy and traffic calming, but they can reduce overall network resilience and routing flexibility.

Location: Carderock Springs, MD


Cul-de-Sac Network Pattern: Quick Pros and Cons
Pros Cons
Very low through-traffic on local residential streets. Few entry/exit points create collector-road bottlenecks.
Supports traffic calming and neighborhood privacy. Poor network redundancy during incidents or closures.
Good fit for quiet, low-speed residential design. Longer emergency, transit, and cross-neighborhood routing.
